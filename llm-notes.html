Parameter-Golf Notes

A working writeup of how we approached the challenge: what seemed promising, what actually moved the number, and which ideas simply did not earn their keep under a brutal training budget.

April 2026 Tokenization Optimizer Test-Time Compute
neurology

This was a joint effort with Tim Shen. Thanks also to Xingyuan Ding for helping with additional experiments, and to Bill (Yiyuan) Li for several meaningful discussions about tokenizers.

I started looking seriously at the challenge around April 20, 2026. At that point the merged leaderboard had mostly stopped moving, and it did not change much after that either.

The first thing I did was analyze the baseline implementation and skim the PRs that existed at the time. There was already a lot of good work in flight. It was exciting, but also honestly a bit overwhelming.

What helped was reducing the task to a sequence of smaller parts:

data -> tokenization -> model architecture -> optimizer -> quantization -> test-time compute

Data

On the data side, one trend showed up very clearly: smaller vocabularies had a low ceiling. Across many of the later successful PRs, 8192 seemed to be the sweet spot.

That said, relying on a SentencePiece tokenizer never felt like the final answer. I kept coming back to the idea that the tokenization scheme needed to preserve enough difficulty. If the task became too easy, this network simply plateaued.

  • I suspected full-word tokenization was wasteful under the challenge's time and compute limits.
  • I tried pruning vocabularies and removing long words that could be reconstructed from shorter subword tokens already in the vocab.
  • That did not help. One run came out roughly +0.001 bpb, which was directionally worse.
  • Lowercasing the data helped, while treating leading capitalization as a special token.
  • I also tried normalizing the dataset by stripping long URLs and other rare, awkward patterns from FineWeb.

Another useful signal was that training on only 1024 tokens kept showing a low ceiling. My interpretation is that tokenization has to make the modeling problem hard enough. If it over-simplifies the sequence, this model family peaks too early.

Stage 5 was a focused tokenizer sweep that put numbers on the <cap> idea — folding capitalized variants back into a single marker plus the lowercase token. Against a vanilla 8192-BPE anchor, the sweet spot landed at p=0.2 inverse-frequency removal (220 tokens dropped, 1.027× sequence-length inflation): -0.00093 bpb, roughly twice the single-seed noise floor. Parameter savings from the smaller embedding bank offset the inflation cost cleanly. A more aggressive p=0.5 traded a little BPB for far more bytes (-0.00050 bpb at -145 KB artifact) — the cleaner pick whenever the size budget is the binding constraint.

Two side findings turned out more interesting than the headline. At p=1.0 (remove every eligible cap token, 1.135× inflation), BPB was still slightly better than the anchor (-0.00020) — the full-removal cost was essentially zero, with parameter savings exactly offsetting the inflation. And uniform random removal underperformed inverse-frequency by only +0.00014 at the same vocab size, which suggests the dominant variable is the inflation ratio itself, not which specific tokens get removed. The corollary: a frequency-gated variant (keep tokens above a 1% frequency floor) was actively worse (+0.00058), because it kept the inflation cost without buying back the parameter savings.

Model Architecture

When I first saw the 9-layer baseline, it looked fairly standard. Depth recurrence was already there, and that felt like one of the community tricks that had already proved itself.

From there I explored whether replacing GQA with MHA might be worth it: spend a little more parameter budget, hope for better quality. I also implemented a more elaborate local attention head. That idea failed badly.

The issue was not just theory, it was systems. The implementation was inherently inefficient and could not take advantage of the optimized FlashAttention-style ecosystem, so whatever benefit it might have had on paper got crushed by practical cost.

I also looked at several fancier ideas: DeepSeek engrams, value embeddings, and embedding factorizations. None of them paid off in this regime. With only about ten minutes of training time, they simply did not become useful fast enough.

Vanilla attention plus MLP was extremely hard to beat under this budget.

If anything reliably helped, it was making the MLPs wider. Most other architectural tweaks did not show enough return on investment.

A later Stage 1 ablation sweep sharpened this picture. The two attention-side gates carried most of the architectural budget on this stack. Removing SparseAttnGate cost +0.00356 bpb, the single largest swing in the sweep. SmearGate was worth another +0.00235 when ablated. The BOS-mask fix that distinguishes the #1851 SmearGate from the original #1797 version was worth +0.00056 on its own, almost exactly the size I had guessed before running the sweep.

Two smaller signals also showed up. Replacing the sparse gate with a dense GatedAttn variant was strictly worse (+0.00180). And re-enabling the GateQuant flag that #1851 had deliberately turned off was essentially free (-0.00011) — a small neutral lever worth keeping in mind.

Stage 4 came back to the MHA question, this time under the strict 16 MB byte cap. Replacing GQA (kv=4) with MHA (kv=8) added KV-cache parameters that immediately overflowed the budget. To bring MHA back inside the cap, I traded either MLP width (MLP_MULT 4.0 → 3.5) or one layer of depth (L=11 → L=10). Both were strictly worse than the GQA point at the same byte budget: MHA_M35 came in at 1.06513 (+0.00374 vs the GQA baseline of 1.06139) and MHA_L10 at 1.06352 (+0.00213). The unconstrained MHA win I had hoped for was being bought entirely by the extra MLP capacity, not by MHA itself.

Two smaller observations from the same sweep. Layer depth gave more BPB headroom than MLP width when shrinking to fit — pushing MLP_MULT from 3.5 down to 3.0 cost +0.00463, confirming that MLP capacity is the real budget bottleneck on this stack. Shifting PARALLEL_START_LAYER from 8 to 7, a no-cost architectural knob, bought another -0.00047. And the Stage 1/2 free wins (LQER top_k=1, GateQuant on, TTT batch_size=16) stacked cleanly on top of MHA_M35 for -0.00277 bpb, bringing the best cap-legal MHA point within +0.00097 of the GQA baseline. That trio appears to be near-additively free across architecture choices.

Optimizer

We first ran ablations between Muon and AdamW. I initially expected AdamW to stay reasonably close on a relatively small dataset. That turned out to be wrong.

In our experiments, AdamW consistently lagged Muon. After that, I spent some time looking inside Muon itself to see where the remaining slack might be.

One target was the all-reduce communication overhead. I eventually got a very small gain from what I was informally calling the 0427 trick, but the improvement was only about 0.0005. Useful, but not game-changing.

A follow-up Stage 2 sweep also poked at the training pipeline itself. Turning torch.compile off entirely crashed at serialization — the surrounding codepath quietly assumed compile-on. Switching the main training compile from static to dynamic=True was strictly worse (+0.00258 bpb vs the compile-on diagnostic baseline). Under a 600s wall-clock cap there was no hidden throughput win in this knob; static compile was already the right default.

Stage 3 pushed on batch size and Muon learning-rate scaling. The wall-clock cap made the headline simpler than I expected: doubling effective batch at a fixed LR reliably hurt. The 1.0x baseline came in at 1.06342 bpb; 1.5x batch at the same LR landed at 1.06570 (+0.00228), because fewer effective updates fit in 600 seconds. The classic sqrt(k) rule helped slightly at 1.5x (-0.00039 vs fixed) and linear LR over-corrected (+0.00057). A sub-sqrt Muon factor looked promising at 1.75x batch, but those larger-batch runs were not iso-step, so a clean rerun is needed before reading too much into them.

The harder boundary was memory. Every 2.0x batch cell OOMed in fused-CE backward on 80 GB H100s, regardless of LR scale. The practical ceiling on this stack was around 1.75x, and even there throughput collapsed (about 2.6 Mtok/s versus 6.6 Mtok/s at 1.0x). Under this budget, larger batch simply was not the right axis to push.

Stage 6 was a profiling-only cell that traced where Muon's per-step time actually goes, and the result quietly reframed the all-reduce intuition. Out of a 4.82 ms training step, exposed collective wait time was only 112 µs, about 2.3% of the step. The all-reduce overhead I had been chasing with the 0427 trick was already well-pipelined; raw wire time was not the bottleneck.

The real cost was hiding inside the optimizer's packed-grad path. The opt.ar_packed phase took 2.77 ms per step (about 57% of the whole step), but the underlying collective only spent 56 µs waiting on wire. The remaining ~2.7 ms was tensor pack/unpack overhead — cat + copy_ of many small gradients inside _all_reduce_packed_grads. That single cat/copy pattern was the actual cap on Muon throughput at this scale; a fused pack (via torch._foreach_* or a custom kernel) could plausibly recover 20-30% throughput at 1.0x batch.

Quantization

Quantization still felt somewhat like a black box to me, even though we had done it before. My intuition was that group quantization should provide a more stable estimate of parameter statistics and therefore be better suited for GPTQ.

In practice, though, the group statistics also take space. That pushed the submission file toward the size limit, and the gain just did not justify the cost.

My intuition still says QAT should work better eventually, but I never got a successful QAT run during this round.

Stage 1 ablations reinforced the harder lesson: quantization is constrained as much by file size as by BPB. LQER in total was worth about +0.00038 bpb when fully ablated — well below the +0.001 to +0.003 range I had loosely predicted. Most of the LQER sub-knobs (sym vs asym, group sizes, factor bits) sat within noise of one another, and rank=8 actively hurt (+0.00077).

One small, real win surfaced: dropping LQER top_k from 3 to 1 improved BPB by 0.00044. The default appears to over-spend compensation on tensors that did not really need it.

The other side of the story was the file-size wall. embed_bits=8 and a looser MLP clip were the two settings that genuinely improved BPB (-0.00080 and -0.00141) — but both pushed the artifact past the size limit. Pushing matrix bits more aggressive (down to 5) was a disaster at +0.0269 bpb. Bit-budget gains and BPB gains are not independent constraints.

A Stage 2 follow-up tested the harder floor. Matrix_bits=4 was unrecoverable: pre-quant BPB jumped to 1.43, and adding LQER rank=8 as a rescue did not bring it back (1.28 vs the 1.06182 anchor). Int4 matrix quantization is past the actual floor for this model size, and LQER compensation is not the bottleneck that is saturating. On the other side, dropping embed_bits from 8 to 6 cost only +0.00080 bpb while shrinking the artifact by 242 KB — one of the cleaner BPB-vs-bytes tradeoff levers in the stack.

Test-Time Compute

Test-time compute felt the most like a backdoor lottery ticket. The space was noisy, and a lot of it felt less principled than the earlier parts of the stack.

The main theme, at least from my perspective, was to align the trained distribution with the test-time distribution as much as possible. Beyond that, many of the gains felt opportunistic rather than deeply structural.

In the Stage 1 sweep, TTT was the place where the numbers most clearly justified the engineering. The baseline post-TTT score (1.06172) sat ~0.013 bpb below the pre-TTT floor (1.07507). That single delta was several times larger than every gated-attention or quantization knob we touched.

Phased TTT preferred three phases: collapsing to one cost +0.00118, while five phases were within noise. The alpha and weight-decay values inherited from S9 were each slightly worse than the #1851 defaults, by small margins.

An unexpected wrinkle: replaying the older #1797 configuration on top of the #1851 stack came out at 1.06149 bpb, very slightly ahead of the #1851 baseline (1.06172). The two changes (BOS-mask off, GateQuant on) interact non-additively — at this resolution, leaderboard ordering is not a clean total order even within a single code lineage.

Stage 2 added one more probe at the TTT inner loop. The hypothesis going in was that small TTT batches should be too noisy to give clean LoRA gradients. The data went the other way. At fixed LoRA rank 96, dropping batch size from 64 to 16 improved post-TTT BPB from 1.06195 to 1.06169. Going to rank 192 with batch size 16 was the best of the four cells (1.06166). The "noisier gradient hurts BPB" story did not hold up here — TTT seemed to prefer more, smaller updates.

Why This Page Exists

I wanted to preserve the false starts, partial intuitions, and small local lessons instead of polishing the process into something tidier than it really was.

Takeaways

  • 8192 vocab was the BPE sweet spot; the <cap>-token trick can shrink it a touch further.
  • Tokenization should not make the task too easy.
  • Attention gates and wider MLPs mattered more than fancier architecture tweaks.
  • Under a fixed byte cap, MLP capacity is the real budget bottleneck — even MHA loses to GQA.
  • Muon beat AdamW more clearly than I expected.
  • Muon's real throughput cap was packed-grad pack/copy, not the all-reduce wire.
  • Quantization gains were real but BPB and byte budget are joint constraints, not independent.
  • TTT was the single largest lever — bigger than every architectural or quantization knob combined.
