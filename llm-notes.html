Parameter-Golf Notes

A working writeup of how we approached the challenge: what seemed promising, what actually moved the number, and which ideas simply did not earn their keep under a brutal training budget.

April 2026 Tokenization Optimizer Test-Time Compute
neurology

This was a joint effort with Tim Shen. Thanks also to Xingyuan Ding for helping with additional experiments, and to Bill (Yiyuan) Li for several meaningful discussions about tokenizers.

I started looking seriously at the challenge around April 20, 2026. At that point the merged leaderboard had mostly stopped moving, and it did not change much after that either.

The first thing I did was analyze the baseline implementation and skim the PRs that existed at the time. There was already a lot of good work in flight. It was exciting, but also honestly a bit overwhelming.

What helped was reducing the task to a sequence of smaller parts:

data -> tokenization -> model architecture -> optimizer -> quantization -> test-time compute

Data

On the data side, one trend showed up very clearly: smaller vocabularies had a low ceiling. Across many of the later successful PRs, 8192 seemed to be the sweet spot.

That said, relying on a SentencePiece tokenizer never felt like the final answer. I kept coming back to the idea that the tokenization scheme needed to preserve enough difficulty. If the task became too easy, this network simply plateaued.

  • I suspected full-word tokenization was wasteful under the challenge's time and compute limits.
  • I tried pruning vocabularies and removing long words that could be reconstructed from shorter subword tokens already in the vocab.
  • That did not help. One run came out roughly +0.001 bpb, which was directionally worse.
  • Lowercasing the data helped, while treating leading capitalization as a special token.
  • I also tried normalizing the dataset by stripping long URLs and other rare, awkward patterns from FineWeb.

Another useful signal was that training on only 1024 tokens kept showing a low ceiling. My interpretation is that tokenization has to make the modeling problem hard enough. If it over-simplifies the sequence, this model family peaks too early.

Model Architecture

When I first saw the 9-layer baseline, it looked fairly standard. Depth recurrence was already there, and that felt like one of the community tricks that had already proved itself.

From there I explored whether replacing GQA with MHA might be worth it: spend a little more parameter budget, hope for better quality. I also implemented a more elaborate local attention head. That idea failed badly.

The issue was not just theory, it was systems. The implementation was inherently inefficient and could not take advantage of the optimized FlashAttention-style ecosystem, so whatever benefit it might have had on paper got crushed by practical cost.

I also looked at several fancier ideas: DeepSeek engrams, value embeddings, and embedding factorizations. None of them paid off in this regime. With only about ten minutes of training time, they simply did not become useful fast enough.

Vanilla attention plus MLP was extremely hard to beat under this budget.

If anything reliably helped, it was making the MLPs wider. Most other architectural tweaks did not show enough return on investment.

Optimizer

We first ran ablations between Muon and AdamW. I initially expected AdamW to stay reasonably close on a relatively small dataset. That turned out to be wrong.

In our experiments, AdamW consistently lagged Muon. After that, I spent some time looking inside Muon itself to see where the remaining slack might be.

One target was the all-reduce communication overhead. I eventually got a very small gain from what I was informally calling the 0427 trick, but the improvement was only about 0.0005. Useful, but not game-changing.

Quantization

Quantization still felt somewhat like a black box to me, even though we had done it before. My intuition was that group quantization should provide a more stable estimate of parameter statistics and therefore be better suited for GPTQ.

In practice, though, the group statistics also take space. That pushed the submission file toward the size limit, and the gain just did not justify the cost.

My intuition still says QAT should work better eventually, but I never got a successful QAT run during this round.

Test-Time Compute

Test-time compute felt the most like a backdoor lottery ticket. The space was noisy, and a lot of it felt less principled than the earlier parts of the stack.

The main theme, at least from my perspective, was to align the trained distribution with the test-time distribution as much as possible. Beyond that, many of the gains felt opportunistic rather than deeply structural.

Why This Page Exists

I wanted to preserve the false starts, partial intuitions, and small local lessons instead of polishing the process into something tidier than it really was.

Takeaways

  • 8192 vocab looked like the best tradeoff.
  • Tokenization should not make the task too easy.
  • Wider MLPs paid off more than fancy architecture tweaks.
  • Muon beat AdamW more clearly than I expected.
  • Quantization gains were real but hard to realize cleanly.
